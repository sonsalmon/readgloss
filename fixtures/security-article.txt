Attackers rarely break cryptography directly. Instead they look for weak implementations, forgotten endpoints, and careless configuration. A typical intrusion begins with reconnaissance: scanning exposed services, fingerprinting software versions, and harvesting credentials leaked through old repositories. Once an adversary finds a vulnerable component, they craft an exploit that delivers a malicious payload to the target process.

Defenders respond by reducing the attack surface. Patching known flaws, disabling unused daemons, and segmenting networks all limit what a compromised host can reach. Logging and telemetry matter too, because detection depends on noticing anomalous behaviour before an intruder establishes persistence. Mature teams rehearse incident response, rotate secrets regularly, and verify backups under realistic conditions.

Injection remains a stubborn class of bugs. When an application concatenates untrusted input into a query, template, or shell command, the interpreter cannot distinguish data from instructions. Parameterized statements, strict allowlists, and contextual output encoding neutralize most variants. Deserialization flaws are similar: reconstructing objects from attacker-controlled bytes can trigger unexpected gadget chains that execute arbitrary code.

Privilege escalation turns a small foothold into full control. A process running with excessive permissions, a writable service binary, or a misconfigured sudoers entry may let a low-privileged account become administrator. Containers promise isolation, yet shared kernels, mounted sockets, and permissive capabilities frequently undermine that promise. Least privilege, immutable images, and read-only filesystems keep the blast radius small.

Browsers add another battleground. Cross-site scripting lets hostile markup run inside a trusted origin, stealing sessions or rewriting pages. Content security policies, strict cookie attributes, and framework-level escaping reduce exposure, although legacy widgets often bypass them. Server-side request forgery abuses features that fetch remote resources, tricking internal systems into contacting metadata endpoints or administrative panels.

Supply chain compromise has grown more common. Maintainers lose accounts, build pipelines inject tampered artifacts, and typosquatted packages wait for hurried developers. Pinning versions, verifying signatures, generating provenance attestations, and reviewing transitive dependencies help organizations notice suspicious changes early. Reproducible builds make silent substitution far harder to conceal.

Finally, people remain central. Phishing campaigns exploit urgency, authority, and curiosity, while social engineers impersonate colleagues to bypass technical controls. Hardware tokens resist credential theft better than passwords or one-time codes. Clear reporting channels, blameless reviews, and steady training encourage employees to flag mistakes quickly, which shortens the window attackers need.
